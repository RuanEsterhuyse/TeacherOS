# TeacherOS Presentation Design Guide

## Purpose and Scope

This guide is the master visual design system for TeacherOS presentations. It governs how an approved instructional presentation specification is translated into a coherent, accessible, classroom-ready deck. It applies across curricula, lessons, subjects, and presentation renderers.

The guide controls visual expression, not instructional content. It must never be used to rewrite objectives, alter lesson sequence, remove required activities, invent assessments, simplify teacher guidance, or substitute new content for an approved presentation specification. When a slide specification and this guide appear to conflict, preserve the specified instructional content and apply the closest compliant visual treatment.

## 1. Teaching Philosophy

TeacherOS creates instructional presentations, not business presentations. A classroom deck is a teaching instrument: it directs attention, supports explanation, makes thinking visible, structures participation, and helps students work with text and evidence.

Every design decision should improve at least one of the following:

- clarity of the learning purpose;
- student access to a text, concept, task, or process;
- the teacher's ability to facilitate instruction;
- the visibility of thinking and evidence;
- productive discussion and collaboration;
- literacy, language development, or independent practice;
- students' ability to follow a sequence without unnecessary cognitive load.

TeacherOS presentations should:

- support teaching rather than compete with the teacher;
- reduce cognitive load by revealing only what students need at that moment;
- emphasize questions, evidence, reasoning, and intellectual work;
- encourage discussion without supplying answers prematurely;
- support reading, writing, speaking, listening, and vocabulary development;
- remain visually clean, calm, and purposeful;
- preserve the distinction between projected student content and private teacher guidance.

The visual system should feel serious, warm, contemporary, and appropriate for Grade 8. It should never look like a sales pitch, a corporate report, an entertainment product, or an elementary-school worksheet.

## 2. General Design Principles

### 2.1 One instructional purpose

Each slide should serve one primary learning objective or instructional move. A slide may contain supporting elements, but they must all contribute to the same purpose. When content requires students to orient, read, discuss, and write as distinct actions, those actions should normally appear as separate instructional moments rather than a crowded multipurpose slide.

### 2.2 One focal point

Every slide must have one immediately recognizable focal point. The focal point may be a question, quotation, image, diagram, model, set of directions, text excerpt, or response frame. Secondary content should support it without competing for attention.

### 2.3 Generous whitespace

Whitespace is an instructional tool. It separates ideas, creates reading order, reduces visual noise, and gives projected content room to breathe. Do not fill empty areas with decoration. Avoid edge-to-edge text, crowded grids, and tightly packed cards.

### 2.4 Clear visual hierarchy

Students should be able to identify the following within three seconds:

1. what the slide is about;
2. what they should look at first;
3. what they should do or think about next.

Use scale, position, weight, contrast, and spacing to establish that hierarchy. Do not rely on color alone.

### 2.5 Large, readable type

All visible text must be readable from the back of a classroom. Use short line lengths, adequate line spacing, and a limited number of text sizes. Never reduce text below the minimum readable size to make excess content fit. Split content or use speaker notes instead.

### 2.6 Consistent spacing

Use a repeatable spacing system for page margins, title-to-content distance, card padding, bullet spacing, and gaps between components. Related elements should sit closer together than unrelated elements. Alignment should be deliberate and consistent.

### 2.7 No unnecessary decoration

Do not add ornamental shapes, stickers, emoji, borders, gradients, patterns, or background imagery unless they communicate meaning or establish a necessary section distinction. Decoration must never compete with student work.

### 2.8 Every visual must support learning

Images, icons, diagrams, charts, maps, and excerpts must clarify content, provide evidence, establish context, model a process, or aid memory. If a visual does not improve understanding, omit it.

### 2.9 Accessible contrast

Text and essential graphics must maintain strong contrast against their backgrounds. Use solid or quiet backgrounds behind text. Avoid text over busy imagery, low-contrast pastels, and color combinations that become indistinguishable for color-blind viewers.

### 2.10 Mature Grade 8 appearance

Use editorial composition, restrained color, authentic imagery, and age-appropriate visual metaphors. Avoid childish clip art, cartoon mascots, bubble lettering, novelty fonts, excessive rounded shapes, and primary-color overload.

### 2.11 Fully editable objects

Text, shapes, tables, charts, diagrams, labels, and layout components should remain independently editable. Do not flatten an entire slide into a single image. When an image is required, keep captions, labels, prompts, and instructional overlays as editable objects.

## 3. Typography

### 3.1 Typeface system

Use a highly legible sans serif for body text and directions. A restrained display or serif face may be used for selected humanities titles when the active theme specifies one, but readability takes priority. Limit a deck to two font families and use the theme's approved fallback fonts.

### 3.2 Title hierarchy

- Deck and section-opening titles: typically 30–40 pt, bold or semibold.
- Standard slide titles: typically 28–34 pt, semibold.
- Titles should normally occupy one or two lines.
- Use sentence case unless a curriculum title requires different capitalization.
- Do not place a period after a short title.

### 3.3 Subtitle hierarchy

- Subtitles: typically 20–24 pt.
- Use regular or medium weight.
- Subtitles should clarify context, purpose, text location, or task mode.
- Maintain clear spacing between title and subtitle.
- Do not use subtitles to hold teacher directions or dense explanation.

### 3.4 Body text

- Body text: typically 18–24 pt; 20 pt is the preferred default.
- Never use body text below 18 pt.
- Use line spacing of approximately 1.15–1.35.
- Keep paragraphs short and avoid full-width lines across a widescreen slide.
- Prefer left alignment for extended reading.
- Center alignment is reserved for short titles, quotations, essential questions, and brief closing prompts.

### 3.5 Captions and labels

- Captions and nonessential labels: typically 12–16 pt.
- Essential labels must be at least 16–18 pt.
- Captions should identify or explain a visual, not introduce a second lesson objective.
- Attribution text may be smaller when required, but it must remain legible and unobtrusive.

### 3.6 Emphasis

- Prefer bold weight for emphasis.
- Use the accent color sparingly for key terms, actions, or evidence labels.
- Italics are appropriate for book titles, brief emphasis, or cited language.
- Avoid underlining except for links or explicit annotation instruction.
- Avoid all caps for sentences; reserve it for very short category labels.
- Never use more than two emphasis methods on the same phrase.

### 3.7 Bullets and lists

- Use no more than three primary bullets by default.
- Keep bullets parallel in structure.
- Use generous vertical spacing between bullets.
- Use sub-bullets only when the hierarchy is instructionally necessary.
- Avoid bullets that wrap beyond two lines.
- Number steps when order matters; use bullets when order does not matter.

### 3.8 Word limits

- Default maximum: 45 visible words per slide.
- Minimal or focal slides: 10–25 words.
- Discussion, direction, and organizer slides: generally 25–45 words.
- A short, source-required excerpt may exceed the default only when the text itself is the object of study.
- Titles, page references, essential labels, and brief footer text still contribute to perceived density.
- Never solve density by shrinking type, removing required content, or paraphrasing approved wording.

## 4. Color System

Color should communicate structure and meaning. It should guide attention, distinguish functional categories, and support recall without becoming decorative noise.

### 4.1 Functional color roles

- **Blue — information:** explanations, context, definitions, reading references, and neutral academic content.
- **Orange — attention:** important directions, cautions, transitions, deadlines, or a single focal action.
- **Green — success:** completion, correct process, progress, reflection on growth, or confirmed understanding.
- **Gray — supporting information:** captions, secondary labels, source references, optional supports, and subdued structural elements.

The active theme may use different exact hues, but these semantic roles should remain consistent within a deck.

### 4.2 Color usage rules

- Use one primary color, one secondary color, one accent color, and a restrained neutral range.
- Maintain a stable background and text color system across the deck.
- Use the accent color on a small proportion of the slide.
- Reserve strong color for information that deserves attention.
- Use tinted surfaces for grouping, not for decoration.
- Do not assign a different bright color to every bullet, card, word, or category.
- Avoid rainbow slides.
- Avoid red/green-only distinctions.
- Pair color with text labels, icons, shapes, patterns, or position when meaning matters.
- Ensure text contrast remains accessible under classroom projection.

## 5. Images and Visual Assets

### 5.1 Quality standard

Images must be sharp, intentional, age-appropriate, and credible at presentation scale. Use modern educational illustrations, realistic classroom visuals, accurate maps, clear diagrams, readable charts, authorized book or curriculum assets, and purposeful text excerpts.

### 5.2 Preferred visual approaches

- modern editorial or educational illustration;
- realistic photography that provides genuine context;
- accurate maps with only necessary labels;
- concept diagrams that show relationships;
- process diagrams that show sequence;
- charts that make comparison or evidence visible;
- authorized excerpts when students must inspect a source;
- simple icons used as navigation or category cues.

### 5.3 Prohibited or discouraged approaches

- childish clip art;
- cartoon mascots or elementary-school visual language;
- decorative stock photography;
- generic images of students pointing at screens;
- culturally stereotyped imagery;
- images that claim to depict a specific text event without source support;
- low-resolution, stretched, distorted, or heavily filtered imagery;
- watermarked or unlicensed assets;
- generated images containing embedded instructional text;
- visual collages that introduce multiple competing focal points.

### 5.4 Visuals should teach

Every visual must perform an instructional function. It should help students observe, locate, compare, infer, sequence, classify, visualize, remember, or discuss. When a visual requires interpretation, pair it with a concise question or direction. When no visual improves learning, use a well-composed text-led slide.

### 5.5 Cropping and placement

- Crop to emphasize the instructional subject.
- Preserve important context and avoid misleading crops.
- Keep faces, labels, and essential details away from slide edges.
- Do not place text over visually complex areas.
- Use full-bleed imagery only when the image is the clear focal point and text remains highly legible.
- Apply supplied alt text to the corresponding asset.

## 6. Slide Types

The following patterns define recommended visual treatments. They do not authorize changes to approved lesson content.

### 6.1 Opening Slide

**Purpose:** Orient students to the lesson, text, topic, day, or instructional arc.

**Recommended layout:** Large title with a concise subtitle or purpose statement; optional single hero visual or restrained thematic background.

**Visual hierarchy:** Lesson title first, instructional context second, date/day/page reference third.

**Recommended visuals:** One meaningful image, book cover when authorized, map detail, conceptual illustration, or no visual.

**Text limits:** Approximately 10–30 words; no agenda paragraphs.

**Teacher considerations:** Establish the lesson purpose verbally. Keep standards, extensive materials, and facilitation notes in speaker notes.

### 6.2 Vocabulary

**Purpose:** Preview, clarify, practice, or revisit words that are necessary for comprehension and expression.

**Recommended layout:** Two to four vocabulary cards, or one featured term with definition, context, morphology, and visual support.

**Visual hierarchy:** Term first, student-useful meaning or task second, example or visual cue third.

**Recommended visuals:** Concept illustration, meaningful icon, morphology diagram, context image, or pronunciation cue.

**Text limits:** No more than four terms per slide; keep definitions concise and source-faithful.

**Teacher considerations:** Do not invent definitions. Keep extended pronunciation guidance, misconceptions, cognates, and ELD supports in speaker notes unless students must see them.

### 6.3 Reading

**Purpose:** Focus attention before or during reading and make the reading task explicit.

**Recommended layout:** Text title/page reference plus one reading focus, tracking question, or annotation direction.

**Visual hierarchy:** Reading location first, purpose or focus second, response expectation third.

**Recommended visuals:** Book or page reference, authorized excerpt, reading-progress marker, evidence tracker, or no visual.

**Text limits:** Usually 20–40 words excluding a short source-required excerpt.

**Teacher considerations:** Do not reproduce long copyrighted passages. Keep scripts, answers, stop points, and detailed questioning in speaker notes.

### 6.4 Teacher Modeling

**Purpose:** Make an expert thinking process, worked example, or academic procedure visible.

**Recommended layout:** Two-column “model” and “thinking” view, annotated example, or stepwise worked process.

**Visual hierarchy:** Example first, teacher action or annotation second, transferable principle third.

**Recommended visuals:** Editable annotation, highlighted evidence, modeled organizer, process arrows, or a before/after comparison.

**Text limits:** One concise model at a time; reveal complex work in steps.

**Teacher considerations:** Preserve productive struggle. Do not display the final answer before the modeled reasoning reaches it.

### 6.5 Discussion

**Purpose:** Invite evidence-based interpretation, reasoning, or exchange of perspectives.

**Recommended layout:** One large discussion question with a brief evidence expectation or response frame.

**Visual hierarchy:** Question first, evidence cue second, participation expectation third.

**Recommended visuals:** Relevant text excerpt, image, diagram, or a restrained discussion icon only when useful.

**Text limits:** One primary question; up to two short supporting prompts.

**Teacher considerations:** Keep anticipated responses, follow-up questions, misconceptions, and facilitation guidance in speaker notes.

### 6.6 Turn and Talk

**Purpose:** Give every student a brief, structured opportunity to rehearse an idea orally.

**Recommended layout:** Prominent prompt with a simple sequence such as Think → Partner A → Partner B → Share.

**Visual hierarchy:** Prompt first, time/grouping second, accountability method third.

**Recommended visuals:** Minimal partner or speech icons, timer cue, or response frame.

**Text limits:** Approximately 15–35 words.

**Teacher considerations:** Specify duration and who speaks first when useful. Keep teacher monitoring notes and expected responses in speaker notes.

### 6.7 Partner Work

**Purpose:** Structure collaborative reading, analysis, practice, comparison, or production.

**Recommended layout:** Numbered steps, two-column partner roles, or shared-task card with a visible product expectation.

**Visual hierarchy:** Task first, roles/process second, deliverable third.

**Recommended visuals:** Role labels, checklist, shared organizer, or source reference.

**Text limits:** Three to five short steps; avoid paragraph directions.

**Teacher considerations:** Clarify accountability and materials. Put grouping adjustments, differentiation, and circulation guidance in speaker notes.

### 6.8 Independent Practice

**Purpose:** Allow students to apply learning independently and provide evidence of understanding.

**Recommended layout:** Clear task, required product, time expectation, and submission/check method.

**Visual hierarchy:** Action verb first, task details second, completion criteria third.

**Recommended visuals:** Worksheet or page reference, progress checklist, response space, or no visual.

**Text limits:** Approximately 20–45 words.

**Teacher considerations:** Avoid showing answers. Put scaffolds, extension options, and observation criteria in speaker notes unless students explicitly need them.

### 6.9 Writing

**Purpose:** Support planning, drafting, revising, editing, or reflecting on written work.

**Recommended layout:** Writing prompt plus success criteria, process step, or sentence frame; use a separate slide for extended rubrics.

**Visual hierarchy:** Writing purpose or prompt first, required elements second, support third.

**Recommended visuals:** Writing-process diagram, planning organizer, model excerpt, checklist, or genre structure.

**Text limits:** One prompt with up to three concise criteria.

**Teacher considerations:** Keep exemplar analysis, conferring notes, misconceptions, and differentiated supports in speaker notes unless students must reference them.

### 6.10 Graphic Organizer

**Purpose:** Make relationships, evidence, sequence, comparison, cause, or reasoning visible.

**Recommended layout:** Editable chart, matrix, flow, timeline, concept map, evidence table, or cause-and-effect structure.

**Visual hierarchy:** Organizer labels first, current focus area second, supporting directions third.

**Recommended visuals:** The organizer itself is the primary visual.

**Text limits:** Short labels and concise entries; do not prefill student thinking unless modeling requires it.

**Teacher considerations:** Preserve enough empty space for student contributions. Reveal sections progressively when the organizer is complex.

### 6.11 Exit Ticket

**Purpose:** Capture concise evidence of learning at the end of an instructional segment.

**Recommended layout:** One response task, optional sentence frame, and clear submission direction.

**Visual hierarchy:** Prompt first, evidence requirement second, response format third.

**Recommended visuals:** Minimal ticket/check icon, response box, or no visual.

**Text limits:** Approximately 15–40 words.

**Teacher considerations:** Keep scoring guidance, sample responses, and follow-up actions in speaker notes.

### 6.12 Assessment

**Purpose:** Communicate an approved formal or formative assessment task without coaching students toward an answer.

**Recommended layout:** Assessment title, exact directions, required materials, timing, and submission expectation.

**Visual hierarchy:** Directions first, constraints second, completion information third.

**Recommended visuals:** Minimal and functional; use source pages, item groups, or progress markers when appropriate.

**Text limits:** Display only the directions students need for the current assessment section.

**Teacher considerations:** Preserve exact assessment wording. Keep answer keys, rubrics not intended for students, administration guidance, and accommodations in speaker notes.

### 6.13 Review

**Purpose:** Retrieve, connect, organize, or check previously learned content.

**Recommended layout:** Retrieval grid, question sequence, comparison, concept map, or concise “What do you remember?” prompt.

**Visual hierarchy:** Retrieval cue first, response mode second, confirmation or connection third.

**Recommended visuals:** Previously used diagrams, timelines, vocabulary categories, or evidence structures.

**Text limits:** Three to five short retrieval cues; avoid dense summary slides.

**Teacher considerations:** Require students to recall before revealing answers. Use stepwise disclosure for confirmation.

### 6.14 Wrap-up

**Purpose:** Consolidate learning, connect back to the objective, preview next steps, or assign approved homework.

**Recommended layout:** One reflection question, concise learning summary, or clearly separated next steps.

**Visual hierarchy:** Reflection or takeaway first, next action second, logistical reminder third.

**Recommended visuals:** Progress marker, quiet thematic image, simple checklist, or no visual.

**Text limits:** Approximately 15–35 words; homework may use up to three concise bullets.

**Teacher considerations:** Keep lesson evaluation, anticipated responses, transition scripting, and follow-up decisions in speaker notes.

## 7. Teacher Notes

Teacher notes never appear on the visible slide.

Teacher notes belong in speaker notes or another renderer-supported private presenter area. Student-facing content and teacher content must remain separate throughout rendering, export, duplication, and revision.

Teacher-only content includes:

- instructional purpose;
- teacher script;
- teacher directions;
- questions not intended for projection;
- anticipated responses;
- misconceptions;
- checks for understanding;
- ELD supports;
- differentiation;
- pacing notes;
- transitions;
- safety or sensitivity guidance;
- answer guidance and assessment administration notes;
- source and fidelity notes that are not intended for students.

Do not summarize, paraphrase, omit, or move teacher notes into the visible slide. If a renderer cannot support speaker notes, preserve the teacher content in a clearly separate teacher-facing artifact rather than projecting it.

## 8. Accessibility

Accessibility is a baseline requirement, not an optional enhancement.

### 8.1 Font size

- Use at least 18 pt for visible body text.
- Prefer 20–24 pt for body text under normal classroom projection.
- Use at least 28 pt for standard slide titles.
- Use at least 16–18 pt for essential labels.
- Never shrink text to resolve overflow.

### 8.2 Contrast

- Maintain strong luminance contrast between text and background.
- Place text on solid or quiet surfaces.
- Add an opaque overlay behind text when imagery is unavoidable.
- Avoid low-contrast gray-on-gray and pastel-on-pastel combinations.

### 8.3 Alt text

- Apply supplied alt text exactly to the corresponding visual.
- Alt text should identify the visual and its instructional purpose when provided.
- Decorative elements should be marked decorative when the renderer supports it.
- Do not replace specific alt text with generic labels such as “image” or “graphic.”

### 8.4 Color blindness

- Do not communicate meaning through color alone.
- Pair color with labels, icons, position, line style, pattern, or shape.
- Avoid red/green-only, blue/purple-only, and other low-distinction comparisons.
- Check charts and diagrams in grayscale when practical.

### 8.5 Screen readability

- Use short lines, clear grouping, and predictable reading order.
- Avoid dense tables and tiny legends.
- Keep essential content within safe margins.
- Preserve logical object order for assistive technologies when supported.

### 8.6 Projection readability

- Design for the back of a classroom, not only for a laptop screen.
- Anticipate lower contrast, ambient light, and imperfect projectors.
- Avoid subtle gradients, fine lines, and small details that disappear at distance.
- Use sufficiently thick strokes and large chart labels.
- Test the slide at reduced size; the focal point and task should remain obvious.

## 9. Animation and Reveal

Animation should support instructional pacing and never become entertainment.

- Use minimal animation.
- Prefer simple appear, fade, or wipe transitions.
- Use stepwise reveals to model a process, control discussion timing, disclose answers after retrieval, or add organizer components in sequence.
- Keep animation direction consistent with reading order.
- Avoid bouncing, spinning, zooming, flashing, random movement, sound effects, and decorative transitions.
- Do not animate every object by default.
- Do not hide information students need to reference throughout a task.
- Ensure the slide remains understandable when animation is unavailable.

## 10. Slide Quality Checklist

A slide is complete only when every applicable item below is satisfied.

### Instructional purpose

- [ ] The slide serves one clear instructional purpose.
- [ ] The content matches the approved lesson objective and sequence.
- [ ] No required content, activity, direction, or assessment has been rewritten or omitted.
- [ ] The slide does not provide answers before students are expected to think.

### Student experience

- [ ] Students can identify what to look at first.
- [ ] Students can identify what to do, think, discuss, read, or write.
- [ ] Directions are concise, direct, and student-facing.
- [ ] Grouping, timing, and accountability are visible when students need them.
- [ ] The slide supports rather than replaces teacher instruction.

### Visual design

- [ ] The slide has one focal point.
- [ ] Visual hierarchy is clear within three seconds.
- [ ] Whitespace is generous and intentional.
- [ ] Alignment and spacing are consistent.
- [ ] Decoration has been removed unless it supports meaning.
- [ ] Every visual contributes to learning.
- [ ] The appearance is mature and appropriate for Grade 8.
- [ ] Objects remain editable.

### Typography and density

- [ ] The title is concise and clearly differentiated.
- [ ] Body text is at least 18 pt and readable under projection.
- [ ] Line lengths and line spacing support quick reading.
- [ ] Bullets are concise, parallel, and adequately spaced.
- [ ] Visible word count is within the specified limit.
- [ ] No text has been shrunk to force content onto the slide.

### Color and imagery

- [ ] Color roles are consistent and meaningful.
- [ ] Contrast is accessible.
- [ ] Meaning does not depend on color alone.
- [ ] Images are relevant, high-quality, accurate, and appropriately licensed.
- [ ] The slide avoids clip art, decorative stock imagery, and stereotypes.
- [ ] Cropping preserves essential context.

### Teacher and student separation

- [ ] No teacher notes appear in visible student content.
- [ ] Teacher scripts, answers, supports, and facilitation guidance remain in speaker notes.
- [ ] Student-facing wording is preserved exactly.
- [ ] Teacher-note wording is preserved exactly.

### Accessibility and technical quality

- [ ] Supplied alt text is attached to the correct visual.
- [ ] Essential labels are large enough to read.
- [ ] The slide remains understandable without animation.
- [ ] Stepwise reveals are used only when instructionally useful.
- [ ] No element is clipped, overlapping, stretched, or outside safe margins.
- [ ] The slide is readable both on screen and under classroom projection.

### Final fidelity check

- [ ] Slide ID, sequence, layout, timing, interaction, and materials match the approved specification.
- [ ] Source references and fidelity classification are preserved.
- [ ] The slide introduces no unsupported claims, quotations, examples, or assessments.
- [ ] The completed slide can be revised without rebuilding it from a flattened image.
